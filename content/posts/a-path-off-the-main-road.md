There’s a lot of dark and scary places on the social internet, but as jaded as I sometimes feel, on some fundamental level, I still believe in its promise — that it can enable new and vibrant forms of human connection. It’s not even that the popular platforms are necessarily bad as a collection of features, but there’s ads, trolls, slop, social engineering — it’s San Diego ComicCon, not a dinner party at my house.

I’ve begun to build basic infrastructure on my project to create a [digital box for family lorekeeping](/photos-post-it-notes-box/). If I want to host a dinner party it turns out that I need to build a road to my house first. I hadn’t really thought about it in concrete dollars and cents before, but websites aren’t free: there’s servers, internet bandwidth, infrastructure, and development costs. Granted, I wasn’t building a theme park for millions of people like a major social media platform, so I didn’t need a highway off-ramp with a parking structure and a shuttle — but I needed a little path off the main road, so that my friends and family could come hang out at my place sometimes.

My server is in my literal garage and I needed some way for my sister in Portland or my auntie in Lafayette to access it. A VPN is basically just a way of connecting two computers, but indirectly — the connection passes through a relay first and a VPN is that relay — or, as I’ve been calling it, a “keyhole.” My home server isn’t open to the whole wide internet, I tell my family, but there’s a tiny thread of a connection that’s locked behind a door and I’m giving them “spare keys” to open it.

But, actually, that’s skipping ahead. There’s a few ways to connect devices that don’t share a network and a VPN wasn’t my first choice. When I got started with self-hosting, the first thing I built was a Jellyfin server from a YouTube tutorial and my first experiments with remote access used Tailscale — a great service provider for making a perimeterless network. But I encountered two problems very early: (1) for what I need, the Tailscale app would need to go on every device, but that’s not always possible; and, (2) Tailscale’s turnkey hosted services are expensive enterprise offerings and the free tier wouldn’t scale across my extended family.

Costs were going to be a thing. When it comes down to it, this project really wouldn’t exist if I could throw money at the problem. It’s not that solutions don’t exist — maybe not perfect ones but very, very good ones — it’s that they’re outside my price range. I am in that awkward position of being fortunate enough to attempt a cheap build, but not so fortunate that I can buy instead. And the more that I’m doing and thinking, the more stubborn I’m feeling about it. What is that — confirmation bias? Whatever, I stand by it.

San Francisco is a weird place because, on the one hand, true wonders of human creation are literally business as usual and, for people in that space, the idea of spinning up custom zero-trust architecture is just, like, whatever, man; on the other hand, I’ve worked with a lot of Bay Area nonprofits, governments, and communities where those tools aren’t accessible either financially or logistically. But why aren’t they?

As a matter of principle, I just don’t think it should be expensive to forge literal (network) connections with your family on the internet. Tailscale’s hosted service was going to be expensive, but a VPN isn’t free either. They’re not that hard to set up, but you need to put it somewhere on the internet. Anyone can rent space on a commercial server, but they work a little like mobile phone plans: you have data limits and incur overage costs depending on your base plan. The cheapest one I found was $5/month and that comes with ~1TB in transfers per month. Because I was running Jellyfin and video uses a lot of data, that could be a problem.

Tailscale doesn’t have that same limitation. It still needs a place on the internet to operate, but the technologies do different things. With a VPN, every byte of data to and from my sister’s TV in Portland and the server in my garage has to pass through that internet place; in a perimeterless network like Tailscale, my sister’s TV asks the internet place to help it find my garage, then the two connect and every byte of data passes between them more or less directly. They’re different methods, but they accomplish similar ends.

I decided to deploy both: a VPN for greater flexibility and security, with Tailscale to manage bandwidth costs. Or, well, kind of Tailscale. As I mentioned, Tailscale’s turnkey solution isn’t priced for hobby projects like mine — but there is an open-source alternative. The alternative allows me to run something like Tailscale’s helper but without Tailscale’s backend, polish, and enriched features. But it’s good enough to make it possible for my sister in Portland to watch Anne of Green Gables as many times as she wants from the computer in my garage without any unpredictable monthly costs.

I now have two little paths off the main road — one built over a VPN tunnel and another built over Tailscale’s coordination pattern. I’m starting to onboard family now and I’m assigning paths based on what their device is capable of and what kind of access I want them to have. I still tell them I’m issuing spare keys, but there’s two different kinds of keys for two different keyholes: one goes to the “front door” and the other goes to a “side door.” Either way, I do feel like I’m inviting them over to hang out sometimes. Maybe my sister and I will SyncPlay Anne over Jellyfin some time and watch it again together, from different states.

That, I think, is the promise of a social internet with community, not shareholder, primacy. As I’m starting to build my own thing, I’m really feeling the friction — and it’s clear why those nonprofits, governments, and communities aren’t able to commit resources toward smoothing it out for themselves. My keyhole solution isn’t elegant, but it does exactly what I need it to do at a low cost that I can accept — and wouldn’t it be cool if everyone felt equipped to develop solutions this way? This is what really activates my stubbornness. I imagine all the amazing things we’d have in the world if we could get these tools in more hands, if more people built and shared, and if more of us were low-key annoying about it until it happens. How else could we develop new ways of being in community across distance?

I’m working on some extra bells and whistles, like a unified admin surface, but I’ve at least proven that I can host something that my sister can access from far away. That’s not yet connection — more like the opportunity for one — but it’s a real first step toward creating my family lore box. Of course, there’s now about 12 major new things on my to-do list, but it’s progress.

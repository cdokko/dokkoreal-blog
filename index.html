Photos, Post-it Notes, Box

Christopher Dokko-Real · September 2026

When my mom came home on hospice, my aunt bought her a digital frame — the kind we could all send pictures to and big enough for her to see without glasses. I was digging through the old albums in her garage, thinking I’d find some photos of her and my aunts as girls that I could scan and add to the frame, when I saw it: a 4x4 color photo of little mom, "1951" scrawled on the back in my grandmother's handwriting. I’d never seen a photo of her as a child in Korea; I didn’t know any from that time had survived. I think a lot of children of immigrants have this same specific gap, so the photograph felt special — an historical treasure that I should be preserving.

Over the next few weeks, I scanned more photos and started texting copies to different group chats. My sister told a story about the shopping bag at her feet from the store in downtown San Francisco that no longer exists. I’m a researcher by practice, so I kept wanting to write everything like this down — just have a million post-its and maybe annotate them in some database later. Just a box to keep all the names, dates, and places that people were bringing up. Memories can surface at weird times but it was happening a lot as we quietly waited to make a big goodbye.

Life was chaotic and messy during those hospice weeks, so I could never have kept track of a literal box with literal post-its. Maybe digital post-its would be okay. How could my sister add a post-it? Could she just write one about the shopping bag and I could keep it in a box and collect all of our post-its? This felt like a design problem, like when I created data collection systems and tooling professionally. I’ve developed surveys and protocols in multiple languages and fielded them to thousands, then developed the standards and processes for the data and analyses on the other side; this was just another data collection problem to solve. I just needed a tool where I could show my family all the photos I was scanning, so that they could write me some post-its, and I’d collect all the post-its together in a box — except, you know, on our phones and not literal post-its.

In these last weeks of her life, the memories seemed so much more important than the things. My mom had some diamond jewelry (originally grandma’s), but we all cared less about those than the stories — with all the names, dates, and places I wanted on all those little post-its. I wondered what post-its mom could’ve written. Maybe the name of the Buddhist monastery where great grandma lived. Or maybe about her life transitioning between Korea and the US, about which I know so little. I wonder what post-its I’d write about my own life — what experiences I’ve had that my niece and nephew might ask about later. The story about the rice bowls that grandma buried and recovered many years later? The house and room that was the last place we were all together as a family before mom died?

Caregiving for mom had taught me that sometimes you just need to do stuff — you know, make your own solutions. My sister and I had been splitting caregiving “shifts” so one of us was always with mom — usually me on days, her on nights. Sometimes I needed to know whether mom could have more pain medication or describe how much she’d been eating to her hospice team, but those things required me to know what happened when I wasn’t there, including precise doses and times; I also had another sister living far away, who wanted visibility into mom’s condition but felt bad asking every single day. We tried handwritten logs, but the notebooks were messy and inconvenient; we tried a whiteboard, but it wasn’t good for keeping the notes day after day. So I stuck little NFC stickers everywhere — my sister and I could tap them to trigger automations that would chart critical information to a shared iCloud calendar, which my other sister could look at from far away. I couldn’t find what we needed, so I made it.

I think it’s important to preserve the evidence of a lived history that leads up to you, that tells the big story in which your life is a chapter, and confronts the ways in which we’re all materially conditioned and historically situated. Especially for people of the immigrant experience, whose family narratives are so often marked by disruption and upheaval, it can be a grounding practice to gather up all this evidence of your belonging — a genealogy of memories that can trace a line from your grandmother’s home to yours, the story of the roots beneath your feet. But I didn’t have the data for that. I didn’t even have a good way of collecting the data for that. I could have the photos, but I needed the post-it notes and I needed the box.

And, boy, did I have photos. I kept on scanning, always at stupid-high DPI resolutions. Over the past couple decades, digital image technology has become increasingly unfathomable to me — with the megapixels and the 4K and the lasers and robots — and I didn’t have the bandwidth to research digital archival best practices, so I erred on too much resolution rather than not enough. I was producing these obscene amounts of data, I was running out of space on Apple iCloud, and my laptop was consistently unhappy. I looked up the price to host this growing behemoth of files for basically forever and, properly horrified, decided to buy a Synology NAS. And, at the time, I thought that maybe Synology Photos could host the photos and collect some comments?

Almost instantly, I realized that was not the solution for me. I’m sure Synology’s ecosystem of apps works great if you’re solving for a handful of people who are often co-located — a nuclear family or a small business office — but I was looking at a different scale. I actually had no idea how many users I might end up having or where in the world they might be. Sure, I could count the family I had Thanksgiving with, but I had cousins in Korea whose names I didn’t really know, and I didn’t want to rule them out at such an early stage of the project. So I started looking at other self-hosted options — and, wow, there were a lot. I found that I could not only replace Synology Photos, but also run wikis, databases, lightweight LLMs — everything I could want to get started on small scale data collection.

Well, everything but the implementation design on the frontend — which I know from experience is the keystone piece. At work, when I’d ask people to give me a bunch of their time and data, I’d pay them — or they’d somehow be compensated. But it’s not like I have an incentives budget to pay the family for whatever lore they possess, so I’ll need to increase their utility in other ways (a fun way to connect with the family over the past) and reduce all the possible pain points in the data collection process (incomprehensible apps or annoying auth). If I expected my sisters and aunties and cousins to write me any post-its, it would have to be easy, familiar, and frictionless for them to do so. But it didn’t make sense to finely tune a process until I could actually host and implement it.

So I decided to spend $200 to #FAFO. I grabbed a used PC from Craigslist and started tinkering. In the homelab space, you can start with a cheap computer — and it was a great way for me to try things out and get the basics. I did a few YouTube tutorials, started a Jellyfin server, and really got bit by the homelab bug. Anyway, I now own a 19” rack enclosure for my Proxmox cluster because that’s just who I am as a person. The point is: I have a server — a place to run all the parts I’d need to share some photos and collect some post-its and put everything into. I have the box.

I registered this website — dokkoreal.com — so I could make the box easy to find. I’ll figure out a cute little subdomain URL that everyone can access, as familiar to my aunties as Facebook. I knew Auntie Patty still checked Facebook in the mornings and Auntie Sunnie was still active on Instagram, even if she only posted photos of her dog; I could persuade them to use a website. The URL would just need to be easy to remember and type into the browser bar. I went with my name because it was short and they’d remember it. I didn’t even opt for a fun TLD — I briefly considered a “.family” address — so that typing it in would feel the most automatic for the most people.

I also started to figure out VPNs because I’m paranoid about data security. I had to think about things like CJIS- and HIPAA-compliance at work, so I couldn’t think about collecting, for everyone in the family, everybody’s mother’s maiden name and the street that they grew up on — common account security questions — without some data safety measure in place. I’m actually starting to set up some WireGuard infrastructure on a virtual private server — a little “keyhole” to my home box, with “spare keys” I’ll give out to family members. (I’m building these metaphors and mental models as I go, because my work trauma means I’m always building the presentation deck in my head.)

A few months ago, my sister moved to Portland — very suddenly vacating the family home. Mom had passed in October and my sister was gone by February. So I found myself living in my mom’s old house — a house my grandpa had originally built — doing physical labor on the daily, patching up holes in the walls or fixing the fence that the neighbors were complaining about. I also ended up upgrading a bunch of my network equipment because wi-fi is garbage in San Francisco as a general rule and I was installing things like cameras and smart doorbells and ESPHome e-ink dashboards. But all the new network things had broken the old server things — and I’ve only started to come back to this family data work recently, as I close out the projects on the house.

I’m in the midst of rebuilding a bunch of stuff now with an actual infrastructure-as-code design so that continuing server work will be more documented and stable moving forward. I’m learning-while-doing in building this box for the family lore. Because this history is still living and evolving in ways I can feel as I paint my own colors over the ones that my mom had chosen, which were themselves painted over the ones my grandmother chose before her. Mom is gone now and I still think about the post-its we’ve already lost — and I’m feeling motivated to keep building a box for them before we lose more. And maybe some day, my niece or my cousin will need this box the way I needed it — but, hopefully, then I can give them one that’s already partly filled.

So many communities throughout time — immigrants, queer folx, people of color — have lost whole histories because there was no safe and reliable mechanism by which to memorialize and archive the evidence of their experiences. They were described by words and measures that didn’t belong to them and were crafted by those who understood them as Other. But what would we know now if their stories had been recorded and kept safe? How might we better understand our own belonging if the places and social relationships that shaped us could be known more deeply? How do we build and steward data about the things that matter to us and with our own words and measures?

No but, like, actually, though… How? How do I turn this pile of hardware into a box that holds a legacy? Because I don’t really know what I’m doing; it’s just me, the internet, and Claude puzzling through right now. My circles wouldn’t generally self-describe as “computer-y” so I’m actively looking for people catching the vibe and might have a helpful word. Could that be you?
